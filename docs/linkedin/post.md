# Companion post

I gave two AIs 50 guesses each to make a jellyfish protein glow brighter.

Both lost to a method that boils down to "copy what nature already does."

The protein is GFP, the green glow that won a Nobel Prize and lights up cells in labs everywhere. In 2016, scientists measured the brightness of more than 50,000 mutated versions of it. That gave me something rare: a complete answer key.

So I built a race. Five strategies, the same 50 guesses, and every guess scored against a real lab measurement. No AI grading AI.

What happened:
→ An AI trained on 65 million protein sequences did worse than picking at random.
→ A chatbot explained every choice in confident, expert-sounding language. 8 of its 50 guesses killed the glow.
→ A simple tally of how GFP's natural relatives are built found the brightest protein, and never once broke it.

The lesson isn't "AI doesn't work." It's that AI is only as good as what it has seen of your specific problem. And sounding right is not the same as being right.

That's true well beyond biology: drug discovery, materials, crops, even which A/B test to run next.
